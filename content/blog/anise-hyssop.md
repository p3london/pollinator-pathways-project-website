**Anise Hyssop** (Agastache foeniculum), though its name suggests otherwise, is neither anise nor Hyssop but belongs to the mint family. Its leaves emit a delicate fragrance similar to anise, with some noting a scent closer to [basil](https://www.thespruce.com/how-to-grow-basil-plants-1402624) or [French tarragon](https://www.thespruce.com/tarragon-care-growing-guide-5024873).

This plant features blue-lavender to purple flower spikes on erect stems with dull green, toothed leaves reminiscent of [common mint](https://www.thespruce.com/growing-mint-1402628).

Anise Hyssop is native to parts of central and northern regions of Canada, ranging from Ontario west to British Columbia, as well as parts of the Northwest Territories and the Prairies. This clump-forming perennial blooms from mid-summer to early fall, drawing bees, butterflies, and hummingbirds. Its prolonged blooming season makes Anise Hyssop ideal for cut flowers and mass plantings.

## Anise Hyssop Care

Plant anise Hyssop in spring after the last frost. Establish seedlings any time until early summer. Space 18 to 24 inches apart in borders, wildflower gardens, herb gardens, or butterfly gardens (or as specimens in containers).

These plants serve well in the middle or the back of perennial borders. Pair with companions such as [Japanese anemone](https://www.thespruce.com/japanese-anemone-4800132), brown-eyed Susan, [goldenrod](https://www.thespruce.com/goldenrod-wildflowers-2132951), or with fellow herbs like [garlic](https://www.thespruce.com/grow-great-garlic-3016629), chives, [oregano](https://www.thespruce.com/growing-oregano-plants-1402818), and [thyme](https://www.thespruce.com/how-to-grow-thyme-1402630).

### Light

Anise Hyssop prefers [full sun](https://www.thespruce.com/what-is-full-sun-partial-shade-1402372). It may grow in partial shade but will get lanky without enough sunlight.

### Soil

Anise Hyssop will do best when planted in soil that is fertile and well-drained. Do your best to avoid soil that is too heavy, which can make it difficult for the roots to get established and may lead to root rot.

Additionally, your soil should have a [pH that is as close to neutral](https://www.thespruce.com/what-to-know-about-soil-ph-5204392) as possible. You can amend it with lime if it's too acidic.

### Water

Water newly planted anise Hyssop weekly, if there is no rainfall, for the first four weeks. Water slowly and deeply, welcoming deep, spreading roots. Once plants are established, cease watering. This perennial is drought-tolerant.

### Fertilizer

Feed anise Hyssop in early spring every other year, with organic compost that has been sprinkled around the base of the plant. Make sure to target the roots and leave a couple of inches of soil space between the compost and the plant's main stem. You can also use [all-purpose fertilizer](https://www.thespruce.com/best-fertilizers-4154249).

## Pruning

Anise hyssop is a fairly independent plant and won't need much attention once it has established itself in your landscape. [Occasional pruning](https://www.thespruce.com/how-and-when-to-prune-plants-1403009) can help encourage the plant to bloom and keep the plant looking its best.

Pruning is best done in early spring, using a pair of clean [pruning shears](https://www.thespruce.com/best-pruners-4154902). Cut back up to one-third of the plant to encourage more full, bushy growth. Remove any dead plant material just above a promising bud node.

## Propagating Anise Hyssop

Every three to five years, you can dig up the plant and divide it. This will help prevent the center from dying out and rejuvenate the entire plant.

1. Divide the plant in spring by digging up the clump and dividing it in half.
2. Replant the division at the same depth as the original planting, at least 2 feet away from the original planting.
3. Water both plants well until re-established.

## Common Plant Diseases

Be mindful that crown/root rot may occur in poorly drained soils. Keep an eye out for rust, powdery mildew, and leaf spots. Despite these potential issues, anise hyssop is quite hardy, being deer-resistant and a generally vigorous perennial.
